📖 Why this single experiment is the whole field

The measurement is the point

Continual learning is not about a clever trick — it's about a metric. After training on B, you re-test on A. The drop in A is forgetting. Every method below is just a different way to keep that drop small without crippling how well B is learned.

What each method does to the curves

  • Naive — only B in the batch. Green collapses toward chance; blue rises fast. Cheap, but the old skill is gone.
  • Replay — mix a fraction of old A samples into every batch. Green is held up because the optimizer still sees A. Slider = how much old data (15–30% is the research sweet spot).
  • EWC — add a penalty that resists changing weights important to A. Green is protected by the penalty strength λ, but high λ makes blue learn slowly (the model is too rigid).
  • Adapters — freeze the base, train a brand-new LoRA for B. A's adapter is untouched on disk → green stays at 100%. Cost: you must know which adapter to load at test time (a router).

The trade-off you're feeling

There is no free lunch: protecting A (high green) usually slows B (low blue), and zero-forgetting adapters add routing cost. The "best" method depends on your budget and how different the tasks are (Case 05 panel 2).

📖 How does replay prevent forgetting?

The idea

Instead of training only on new data, mix in a small fraction of representative old samples. The optimizer must find weights that work for both distributions at once.

What the bottom landscape means

Each "valley" is a region of weights that drives one task well. With pure-B training the ball rolls into B's valley and leaves A's. Replay reshapes the combined loss so the lowest point sits in the overlap — good enough for both.

Production note

Replay is the strongest baseline in the literature (tutorial §2, §9). Many fancier methods lose to a well-tuned replay buffer. The cost is storage (you must keep old samples) and slightly slower B-learning.

📖 What is EWC and Fisher information?

Elastic Weight Consolidation

After learning A, EWC estimates which parameters matter most for A — their Fisher information F. During B-training it adds a penalty for moving those weights:

Loss = Loss_B + (λ/2) · Σ F_i · (θ_i − θ*_i)²

θ*_i is the old optimal value. A high-F weight gets a stiff spring; a low-F weight is free to move.

What the λ slider does

  • λ = 0 → no spring → pure B → forget A.
  • λ moderate → weight settles between A and B → both okay.
  • λ huge → weight frozen at A → B never learned.

Limitation

EWC constrains the parameter space, so it protects A but doesn't teach B as fully as replay. That's why replay usually wins when you can afford to store old data.

📖 How do per-task adapters eliminate forgetting?

The architecture

Train adapter A, save it; train a fresh adapter B, save it; train C, save it. Each is a tiny file (tens of KB for small rank). Because A's file is never re-opened during B-training, A cannot be forgotten.

The catch: routing

At inference you must load the right adapter. If you know the situation, easy. If not, you need a router (learned or heuristic). A wrong route = the wrong expert answers — the failure mode the checkbox simulates.

Trade-offs

  • Pros: zero forgetting, adapters independent, cheap to store thousands (MLSys 2024 in tutorial).
  • Cons: need task ID / router at test time; no cross-task generalization inside one adapter.

Connection to the capstone
